## Abstract

### Abstract

Detecting atomicity violations effectively in interrupt-driven programs is difficult due to the asymmetric concurrency interleaving of interrupts. Current approaches face two main challenges: (1) A large number of false positives are generated by efficient static analysis techniques. (2) Loops with large or unknown bounds in these programs limit the scalability of the bounded verification techniques. To address these challenges, we present NIChecker, a new bounded verification tool designed to detect atomicity violations in interrupt-driven programs. The key ideas are: (1) Transforming an interrupt-driven program into a bounded sequential C program through lazy sequentialization technique. This sequential program accurately models interrupt masking and nested interrupt execution. (2) Combining a refined loop abstraction technique with our sequentialization to enhance the efficiency of detecting programs with intractable loops. (3) Integrating slicing and an interleaving path reduction technique known as preemption point reduction in NIChecker to shrink the explored state space. We prove the bounded correctness of our translation and discuss the impact of our optimizations. We evaluate NIChecker on 31 academic benchmark programs and 18 real-world interrupt-driven programs. Our results show that NIChecker achieves better precision, a lower false positive rate, and a significant verification speed-up than related state-of-the-art tools.

## 1 Introduction

Interrupt-driven programs are commonly used in safety-critical embedded systems, such as aerospace, traffic management, and medical equipment, that heavily depend on interrupts to implement concurrency. However, atomicity violations, the most typical category of concurrency bugs,[^1] often lead to serious safety problems in these programs \[[^30], [^49]\]. Detecting concurrency bugs in interrupt-driven programs is challenging in practice. The main difficulty is that one must consider both complex asymmetric preemption semantics of interrupts (nested interrupts) and enormous concurrent interleavings. Moreover, since loops are usually used to perform various operations in interrupt-driven programs, such as traversing data structures and controlling conditional execution, these loops, especially those with intractably large or unknown bounds, further complicate the interleaving paths of the programs.

Existing techniques for detecting concurrency bugs in interrupt-driven programs mainly contain testing, static analysis, and model checking. Techniques such as testing tend to miss bugs and hardly reproduce detected bugs as the number of interrupt responses grows; thus, they are unreliable for analyzing nested interrupts \[[^31]\]. Although most static analysis methods are efficient for finding potential concurrency bugs, there can be a large number of **false positives (FP)** in these bugs since static analysis usually ignores, for efficiency purposes, path conditions and variable information of programs (e.g., \[[^20], [^30]\]). Manual inspection of these FPs is costly, which restricts the applicability of these methods in practice. In contrast, **bounded model checking (BMC)** technique is good at generating counterexamples that can help better aid the manual validation \[[^18]\]. The basic idea in BMC is to find bugs in executions whose length is bounded by some integer *u* \[[^10]\]. In theory, BMC-based verification techniques can find bugs hidden deep in the loop (deep bugs) by iteratively increasing *u*. Unfortunately, they often do not scale well for large *u* since the number of states detected by SAT/SMT solvers grows exponentially as *u* increases. **Loop abstraction (LA)**, as proposed by Darke et al. \[[^17]\], is an abstract acceleration technique that is ideal for handling programs with intractable loops. LA replaces a given loop with an over-appropriated loop that contains a small known bound, which makes it possible for BMC to detect deep bugs with a smaller bound *u*. However, to our knowledge, there are currently no techniques or tools that take advantage of LA as an auxiliary technique for detecting atomicity violations of nested interrupts. Overall, there is still a lack of techniques or tools for effectively verifying interrupt-driven programs.

The concurrent behaviors manifested by interrupts can be viewed roughly as a subset of those displayed by concurrent multi-threaded programs \[[^41]\]. Thus, it is worth trying to solve the problem of interrupt-driven programs by using off-the-shelf detection techniques for concurrent programs. **Lazy sequentialization** **(LazySeq)** proposed by Inverso et al. \[[^24] – [^26]\] is an effective bounded sequentialization technique for multi-threaded programs. Its implementation tool, Lazy-CSeq \[[^24] – [^26]\], has won gold prizes in the TACAS International Competition on Software Verification (SV-COMP) in the “Concurrency Safety” category \[[^7] – [^9]\]. The key to Lazy-CSeq's efficiency is that lazy techniques are combined with context-bounded analysis to explore reachable states only instead of exploring the entire state space \[[^29]\]. However, Lazy-CSeq does not work well for verifying nested interrupts directly because both programs are fundamentally different \[[^39]\] mainly as follows: (i) Asymmetric preemption semantics are the norm for interrupts, while threads are symmetric; (ii) Interrupts can be masked, but threads cannot; (iii) Interrupts cannot block, but threads can. Additionally, Lazy-CSeq is also based on BMC and thus has the same performance problem as those existing bounded methods for interrupt-driven programs.

Inspired by the idea of Lazy-CSeq, we have extended this approach to develop an automatic tool *NIChecker*, which refines LazySeq and incorporates three optimizations to detect concurrency bugs of interrupt-driven programs. First, NIChecker preprocesses an input interrupt-driven C program and outputs a **bounded interrupt-driven program (BIDP)**, which includes bounding the number of executions of each ISR, inlining function calls, and unwinding loops with a bound *u* \[[^16], [^24], [^26]\]. We use a refined LA (one of the optimizations) to replace the loops in the input program with abstract versions before the unwinding operation starts. Since NIChecker is a BMC-based tool, the combined LA helps NIChecker detect deep bugs with a smaller bound. Second, NIChecker employs LazySeq to translate the bounded program into a nondeterministic sequential C program that can simulate a large subset of executions of the input program in a round-robin schedule schema. The **sequential program (SP)** consists of a main driver and finite entry functions that simulate the main task and ISRs of an interrupt-driven program. In each round, the main driver calls each interrupt simulation function that satisfies *invocation conditions* to execute nondeterministic lines of its code, where the invocation conditions take account of interrupt priority and interrupt masking, and the highest-priority function can be executed atomically (i.e., non-interruptable) by designing no context switches in its code. We design an algorithm that automatically inserts auxiliary code in the SP for detecting atomicity violations. Finally, NIChecker utilizes the high-performance tool CBMC \[[^16]\] as the verification backend to detect the properties described as assertions in the output SP. Moreover, we employ two additional optimization techniques to enhance the performance of NIChecker: slicing and **preemption point reduction (PPR)**. The former reduces the detection code scale, while the latter decreases the number of concurrent interleavings.

We evaluated NIChecker on 31 academic benchmark programs and 18 real-world embedded programs, comparing it experimentally with state-of-the-art tools CPA4AV \[[^49]\], iCBMC \[[^31]\], intAtom \[[^30]\], and Rchecker \[[^20]\]. The results over the academic benchmark indicate that NIChecker exhibits the highest precision and the lowest FP rate among all comparison tools. NIChecker is capable of detecting deep bugs in interrupt-driven programs with the benefit of incorporating LA while existing bounded tools cannot detect these bugs. The slicing and PPR techniques effectively reduce the state space explored by the verification backend, consequently enhancing verification efficiency. In the experimental results from real-world programs, NIChecker achieves a maximum speed-up rate of 42.2% in verification runtime with these two optimizations.

In summary, this article makes the following contributions:

- We present an effective LazySeq verification approach for detecting atomicity violations and user-defined assertions in interrupt-driven programs.
- We integrate three optimization techniques into our approach, including LA, slicing, and PPR. Experimental results show that these optimization methods have significantly improved the verification effectiveness and efficiency.
- We develop NIChecker, an automated tool, to implement the proposed approach. We evaluate this tool using 31 academic benchmark programs and 18 real-world benchmark programs and provide a detailed discussion of the correctness of the proposed approach.

## 2 Preliminaries

In this section, we first present the interrupt semantics and the assumptions made throughout this article. Then, we describe the definition of atomicity violations and illustrate a motivating example.

### 2.1 Interrupt-Driven Programs

In interrupt-driven embedded systems, *interrupt response* refers to the processor's actions in response to an interrupt request. These actions include suspending current activities, saving interrupt status, and invoking an interrupt handler (or an ISR). Once the interrupt is handled and completes its execution, the processor resumes its previous activities. Interrupt response only happens if the requesting interrupt is enabled and has a higher priority than the currently executing code. Multiple interrupt responses occur when the processor repeatedly addresses requests from a single interrupt, resulting in multiple invocations of the corresponding ISR. An interrupt can be masked (i.e., *interrupt masking*) when its corresponding disable statement is executed, and thus cannot be responded to by the processor until its corresponding enable statement is executed.

We consider an interrupt-driven program (see [Figure 2](#fig2) for an example) written in C consisting of a main task and a finite number of ISRs. The main task is the only entry procedure (i.e., *main* procedure) of this program; It consists of an infinite *while* loop that contains a finite number of subtasks within the loop. ISRs refer to interrupt service routines, and every ISR has a fixed priority.[^2] The execution of an interrupt-driven program starts with the main task. Initially, we assume all interrupts of the program are enabled. All enabled interrupts and the main task follow *asymmetric preemption semantics*: (i) Each interrupt can preempt the execution of the main task; (ii) A higher-priority interrupt can preempt a lower-priority interrupt, but not vice versa. Note, however, that interrupts of the same priority cannot preempt each other and so they need to be executed sequentially.

Formally, let an interrupt-driven program $P$ be a finite set of $\left\{{{\textit{task}}} , I S R_{1} , \ldots , I S R_{n}\right\}$, where *task* represents the main task, and each $I S R_{i}$ represents an interrupt handler within the program. Without loss of generality, we assume that the priority of $I S R_{i}$ is a positive integer $p_{i}$ for $i \in \left\{1 , 2 , \ldots , n\right\}$. We also assign a priority $p_{0}$ to the *task*, where $p_{0}$ is 0 by default. The priority of each element of $P_{I}$ satisfies that $p_{0} < p_{1} \leq \hdots \leq p_{n}$. The interrupt disable statement, *disable\_isr* ($t$), is employed to mask $I S R_{t}$, but masks all ISRs when $t = - 1$. The interrupt enable statement *enable\_isr* ($t$) performs the opposite function of *disable\_isr* ($t$), unmasking the specific ISR or all ISRs.

For clarity of presentation, we make no distinction between tasks and ISRs and refer to them collectively as concurrent flows of the interrupt-driven program. We categorize the asymmetric preemption semantics of these priority-based concurrent flows of an interrupt-driven program into three behaviors: passive preemption, changeable preemption, and active preemption. [Table 1](#tab1) illustrates these behaviors, explaining how these behaviors occur, and the concurrent flows exhibiting them, with priority levels. For instance, as illustrated in [Figure 2](#fig2), the preemption behaviors of the main task, $I S R_{1}$, and $I S R_{2}$ correspond to passive, changeable, and active preemption, respectively. We have the following observations: (1) the main task always has the lowest priority. (2) At least one ISR has the highest priority in an interrupt-driven program. These observations indicate that both passive preemption and active preemption are always present in the program. Note that there is no concurrent flow with changeable preemption when an interrupt-driven program contains only two levels of priority, e.g., a program consists of a main task and an ISR. Moreover, since interrupt masking also affects the preemption behaviors of interrupts, we assume here that all interrupts are enabled and discuss more complex situations involving interrupt masking in Section [3](#sec-3).

In this article, we assume that the concurrent flows of an interrupt-driven program share the same memory address space, i.e., they communicate by writing to or reading from shared (or global) variables in the program. Statements that perform read or write operations on shared variables are called *visible statements*, while other statements are invisible. There is no interrupt reentrance or recursive functions in interrupt-driven programs because we use inlining during code-to-code translation. We assume that all jump targets of goto-statements are forward.

### 2.2 Atomicity Violation

Atomicity violations are the most encountered interrupt concurrency bugs in interrupt-driven programs \[[^12], [^30]\]. In designing programs, programmers often assume that the execution of some code regions is atomic. Atomicity violations usually occur when an unexpected concurrent access (a *remote access*) breaks the atomicity of these code regions (*two consecutive local accesses*), which may cause unexpected program behaviors \[[^33]\]. *Access interleaving patterns* are defined to capture the situation in which interrupts break the atomicity of code in interrupt-driven programs \[[^20], [^30], [^49]\]. Below, we first introduce shared accesses and atomic statements and then describe access interleaving patterns based on them.

Let symbols $v$, $e x p r$, and $i d e n t$ denote a shared variable, an expression, and a string, respectively. An assignment $v = e x p r ;$ denotes a shared write access to $v$. The assignment $v = e x p r ;$ is an *atomic write access* denoted by the symbol $w_{v}$ iff $v$ does not appear in the expression $e x p r$. We consider an assignment $i d e n t := e x p r$ as shared read accesses concerning all shared variables that appear in the expression $e x p r$. The assignment $i d e n t := e x p r$ is an *atomic read access* denoted by the symbol $r_{v}$ concerning $v$ iff $i d e n t$ is not $v$ and $v$ occurs only once in the expression $e x p r$. Note that it is always possible to decompose a statement into a sequence of atomic statements, each involving at most one shared variable \[[^34]\]. For example, the statement $v = v + 1 ;$ is not atomic access concerning $v$, but it can be split into an atomic read access $t m p = v + 1 ;$ and an atomic write access $v = t m p ;$, where $t m p$ denotes a temporary local variable.

*Access Interleaving Patterns*. Nondeterministic interrupts may preempt and affect the expected execution of atomic code regions through shared accesses (read/write) on shared variables. Let $t_{l}$ be a main task or an ISR and $t_{h}$ be an ISR. The priorities of $t_{l}$ and $t_{h}$ satisfy that $p \left(t_{l}\right) < p \left(t_{h}\right)$. Suppose that $e_{p}$ and $e_{c}$ are two consecutive accesses on a shared variable $v$ in $t_{l}$, $e_{r}$ is a remote access on $v$ in $t_{h}$, and $e_{p} , e_{r} , e_{c} \in \left\{r_{v} , w_{v}\right\}$. There are eight cases for a tuple ($e_{p} , e_{r} , e_{c}$) when the execution of $e_{r}$ in $t_{h}$ occurs between $e_{p}$ and $e_{c}$ in $t_{l}$. Since atomicity violation bugs typically arise from unserializable interleavings rather than serializable ones \[[^33]\], only four such unserializable cases for the tuple ($e_{p} , e_{r} , e_{c}$), termed *access interleaving patterns*, are needed to cover these bugs. We illustrate these patterns in [Figure 1](#fig1) and give their descriptions as follows:

- ($r_{v} , w_{v} , r_{v}$): Accesses $e_{p}$ and $e_{c}$ are expected to read the same value, while $e_{r}$ breaks this expectation.
- ($w_{v} , w_{v} , r_{v}$): Access $e_{c}$ is expected to read the assignment result of $e_{p}$, but $e_{r}$ from a higher-priority interrupt overwrites the expected value of $e_{p}$.
- ($r_{v} , w_{v} , w_{v}$): A typical scenario is a write access $e_{c}$ under a branch condition dependent on the value from the preceding read access $e_{p}$, which is then overwritten by $e_{r}$.
- ($w_{v} , r_{v} , w_{v}$): A violation occurs when the intermediate result of two continuous write accesses, $e_{p}$ and $e_{c}$, remains invisible to other ISRs but is read by access $e_{r}$ in a higher-priority interrupt.

Four access interleaving patterns.

We define patterns ($r_{v} , w_{v} , w_{v}$) and ($w_{v} , w_{v} , r_{v}$) as *symmetric*, since interchanging $r_{v}$ of $e_{p}$ and $w_{v}$ of $e_{c}$ in pattern ($r_{v} , w_{v} , w_{v}$) yields pattern ($w_{v} , w_{v} , r_{v}$), and vice versa. A feasible execution that causes an atomicity violation is not limited to only two concurrent flows. It can also be the nested execution of multiple concurrent flows (see [Figure 2](#fig2) for an example).

A feasible path from $e_{p}$ to $e_{c}$ is $m a i n t a s k \left\{e_{p}\right\} \rightarrow I S R_{1} \rightarrow I S R_{2} \left\{\hdots \rightarrow e_{r} \hdots \rightarrow e x i t\right\} \rightarrow m a i n t a s k \left\{e_{c}\right\}$.

Generally, the execution of the program in [Figure 2](#fig2) starts with the main task and satisfies the preemption semantics: $I S R_{2}$ can preempt the execution of $I S R_{1}$ because the priorities of two ISRs satisfy that $p \left(I S R_{1}\right) < p \left(I S R_{2}\right)$; both ISRs can preempt the main task, but not vice versa. ### 3.2 Lazy Sequentialization for BIDP

We first briefly recall the thread translation of LazySeq as presented by Lazy-CSeq \[[^24] – [^26]\]. Lazy-CSeq first transforms a multi-threaded program into a bounded program by inlining function calls and unwinding loops \[[^16]\]. Then, it injects control code into the bounded program to generate an SP (i.e., executes a code-to-code translation based on LazySeq). The resulting SP consists of a main driver (*main* () function) and thread simulation functions: The original *main* () function containing *pthread\_create* functions is treated as a new thread, and each thread of the bounded program is translated into a thread simulation function. Each thread simulation function contains control code such as numeric labels, conditional goto-statements, and assumptions for controlling multiple hops. The main driver contains a round-robin scheduler. It executes nondeterministic lines of code for each created thread simulation function in a round-robin way. In each round, each active thread simulation function executes zero or more statements of its code, as managed by the control code. This round-based iteration can simulate the symmetric concurrent interleaving execution of threads.

We extended the concurrency control of the original LazySeq to support the concurrency control of interrupts by incorporating modifications to the original control code. As illustrated in [Figure 5](#fig5), our extended LazySeq comprises two main phases: BIDP generation and SP generation. In the first phase, an interrupt-driven program is translated into a BIDP by applying initial translation techniques tailored for handling interrupts. In the second phase, we translate the BIDP into an SP by inserting specially designed control code to simulate the execution of interrupts. Below, we describe each phase in detail.

#### 3.2.1 BIDP Generation.

Unlike multi-threaded programs, where threads are explicitly created using functions like *pthread\_create*, interrupts occur spontaneously and do not require a specific function for their initiation. Furthermore, the occurrence of interrupts is arbitrary, potentially resulting in a large number of executions for each corresponding ISR. In contrast, each thread in a multi-threaded program is typically designed to complete its task and terminate, although it can be executed repeatedly depending on the program's design. In this article, we focus on the bounded verification of interrupt-driven programs and thus bound the number of interrupt responses for each interrupt. In the context of BMC, a BIDP $P$ that consists of $n + 1$ functions $f_{0} , \ldots , f_{n}$ is derived from an unbounded interrupt-driven program $P_{I}$ by applying specific transformations. These transformations include inlining all function calls and unwinding loops with a positive integer $u$, both standard operations in BMC as discussed by Clarke et al. \[[^16]\]. Additionally, the number of executions for each ISR is limited to a positive integer $ℓ$. To simulate multiple responses to an interrupt, a finite while loop is inserted outside the ISR's function body. The structure takes the form:

$$
\begin{aligned}
v o i d I S R \left(\right) \left\{i n t L = 0 ; w h i l e \left(L < ℓ\right) \left\{\left\{f u n c t i o n b o d y\right\} L + + ;\right\}\right\} .
\end{aligned}
$$

This ensures that the interrupt is executed no more than $ℓ$ times. By default, $ℓ$ is set to 1, meaning each ISR is executed at most once unless specified otherwise. To simplify the analysis, we do not insert the while loop when $ℓ = 1$. As shown in [Figure 5](#fig5), function $f_{0}$ simulates the original *main* () function (i.e., $t a s k$) of the interrupt-driven program. Each $I S R_{i}$ associates with a bounded function $f_{i}$.

We now define a $k$ -round-robin execution for the bound program $P$ with a positive integer $k$. Let $\rho$ denote a permutation of functions in $P$. Without loss of generality, we assume $\rho$ is a fixed sequence of functions represented as $f_{0} f_{1} \ldots f_{n}$. An execution $\pi$ of the bounded program $P$ can be divided into a finite sequence of context switches, denoted by $\pi = \pi_{0} \rightsquigarrow \pi_{1} \hdots \rightsquigarrow \pi_{n}$. Each $\pi_{i}$ represents a context associated with a serial execution of a function $f_{j}$ from $P$. The notation $\pi_{i} \rightsquigarrow \pi_{i + 1}$ indicates a context switch from $\pi_{i}$ to $\pi_{i + 1}$. We assume context switches occur solely at shared access statements or upon the completion of a function's execution \[[^26]\]. It is noted that context switches between different functions occur only after a function completes or when a higher-priority interrupt is enabled. For clarity, we assume that adjacent contexts $\pi_{i}$ and $\pi_{i + 1}$ represent contexts of distinct functions; however, these contexts can be merged when they correspond to the same function. An execution $\pi$ is a $k$ *\-round-robin schedule* of $P$ if, for any $i \in \left\{0 , 1 , \ldots , n\right\}$, the number of context switches of $f_{i}$ is limited to $k$, and the sequence of functions represented by each $\pi_{i}$ forms a subsequence of $\rho$ in each round.

### 3.4 Optimization Methods

#### 3.4.1 LA.

BMC-based methods, in theory, can find a deep bug by iteratively increasing unwinding bounds until reaching the bug. They may suffer from the state-space explosion problem caused by larger unwinding bounds. Our approach is also based on BMC and thus faces the problem. For example, [Figure 9](#fig9) shows a simplified program that contains a deep bug requiring an unwinding bound of 10,000.[^3] In order to find this bug, NIChecker requires more time and memory consumption because of the large bound. **LA for bounded model checking (LABMC)**, proposed by Darke et al. \[[^17]\], makes it easier for BMC to find deep bugs with a small unwinding bound. Chimdyalwar et al. \[[^13]\] further extended LABMC to support abstracting loops that modify array contents and aliases by providing nondeterministic values for those modified variables in loops. Inspired by the existing work \[[^13], [^17]\], we first propose to apply the above LA technique to LazySeq.

#### 3.4.2 Slicing.

Slicing is a static program analysis technique that allows programmers to extract program portions relevant to the analyzed property \[[^15]\]. Traditionally, a slicer computes the **program dependence graph (PDG)** of an SP based on a slicing criterion. Subsequently, it traverses the PDG to retain the statements associated with the slicing criterion, producing a corresponding program slice that can be input to a verifier \[[^21]\].

Computing PDGs for interrupt-driven programs is significantly more complex because the data and control dependencies between the main task and ISRs of interrupt-driven programs are not obvious. Interrupt-driven programs mainly differ from SPs in two ways: (1) Interrupts are triggered spontaneously by the external environment rather than by explicit calls to traditional functions. (2) The execution of interrupt-driven programs is concurrent, and the main task and ISRs communicate by shared accesses instead of passing parameters between functions. Wu et al. \[[^47]\] proposed a sequentialization for interrupts by adding an explicit *Schedule* (); function in front of every shared access statement. These added schedule functions make the dependencies between concurrent flows visible. Based on the insight above and inspired by \[[^47]\], we construct the dependencies between the main task and ISRs to facilitate the computation of the PDG for an interrupt-driven program.

We specifically inject an explicit schedule function $\left\{\right.$ *if* (*nondet*) *isr\_i* ();$\left.\right\}$ of $I S R_{i}$ only in front of each read access statement concerning the global variable $v$, instead of injecting the schedule function before every shared access statement. Fewer schedule functions reduce the complexity of PDGs, especially when handling large programs. The data flow dependency of the resulting medium program covers that of the original interrupt-driven program. Essentially, the added schedule functions over-approximate the behavior of the interrupt-driven program. We consider all possible accesses where ISR changes the value of $v$ except for interrupt masking functions (*disable\_isr* ($t$) and *enable\_isr* ($t$)). Thus, we can obtain a sound program slice by feeding the medium program to our slicer. The slicer first computes the PDG and then prunes the interrupt-driven program using a slicing criterion. Note that in the output program slice, we remove all the schedule functions we add in the medium program.

#### 3.4.3 PPR.

In this article, we refer to those context switches in non-highest priority simulation functions as preemption points. Intuitively, many preemption interleavings that could lead to an atomicity violation, defined by the tuple ($e_{p}$, $e_{r}$, $e_{c}$), are equivalent. This equivalence arises because the preemption points, located between shared accesses $e_{p}$ and $e_{c}$ in a lower-priority concurrent flow, are replaceable. In other words, selecting any of these preemption points does not alter the outcome of reaching an atomicity violation. Consequently, analyzing just one representative preemption point effectively provides insights into its entire equivalence class. This observation suggests that analyzing atomicity violations in an interrupt-driven program can be significantly simplified by focusing on a representative subset of the preemption point. Building on this concept, and inspired by the representative preemption point selection discussed in \[[^30]\], we develop a new optimization method, termed PPR, to reduce the number of preemption points considered. We integrate this method into our LazySeq to increase the efficiency of NIChecker in detecting atomicity violations. Recall that, as described in Section [3.2](#sec-3-2), we use the numerical label followed by the macro J(A, B) to achieve the context switches between different simulation functions in our basic translation. Therefore, the key idea to achieve the PPR is reducing the number of inserted numerical labels in interrupt simulation functions.

During the translation using the configuration of LazySeq and PPR, everything remains essentially the same as in the original translation described in Section [3.2](#sec-3-2), except for the handling of numerical labels. Below, we introduce specific conditions for reducing the number of numerical labels when translating interrupt simulation functions. They are used to handle functions whose priority is not the highest (there is no preemption point in the highest priority function). Assume we detect atomicity violations concerning the shared variable $v$. We perform a lightweight static analysis; it first extracts read and write accesses to all shared variables of every function and then computes the access dependencies between functions based on these shared accesses. For any function $f_{i}$ whose priority is not the highest, we continue to insert a numerical label at the beginning and the end of $f_{i}$. Numerical labels are also placed before loop unwinding assumptions. Additionally, we insert a numerical label behind the program statement $s t m t$ of $f_{i}$ when $s t m t$ meets any of the following conditions:

- *Variable access based on atomicity violation patterns*. $s t m t$ accesses the variable $v$:
	- It is a read access if the detection pattern is either $\left(r_{v} , w_{v} , r_{v}\right)$ or $\left(r_{v} , w_{v} , w_{v}\right)$.
		- It is a write access if the detection pattern is either $\left(w_{v} , w_{v} , r_{v}\right)$ or $\left(w_{v} , r_{v} , w_{v}\right)$.
- *Shared access with dependencies*. $s t m t$ is a write access in $f_{i}$ to a shared variable that is also accessed by a higher priority function.
- *Interrupt unmasking control*. $s t m t$ is an interrupt enable function, i.e., *enable\_isr(t)*.

For instance, we present a simple example code and its SP fragment (translated with PPR) in [Figure 11(a)](#fig11) and (b), respectively. The example program contains a main function and an ISR, with all variables being global and initially set to 0. There is an atomicity violation concerning $v$ in the form of pattern $\left(r_{v} , w_{v} , r_{v}\right)$ in the example code. In [Figure 11(b)](#fig11), we can see that there could be a total of four preemption points between the shared accesses $e_{p}$ and $e_{c}$, but only one point located between the statements x=1; and if(m==0) can lead to the violation. By applying PPR, two of these four points (the points following the y=1; and if(m==0) statements) are removed, which means that the number of interleavings considered by CBMC is also reduced. In this example, using PPR does not affect the reachability of the violation, as it does not remove the point that leads to the violation.

## 4 Implementation

We implemented the proposed verification approach of Section [3](#sec-3) into a prototype tool called NIChecker (“Nested-Interrupt-Checker”). This tool is built upon Lazy-CSeq (v2.1) \[[^25], [^26]\], a concurrent program verification tool developed using the CSeq framework \[[^22]\]. As illustrated in [Figure 4](#fig4), NIChecker first integrates a series of software modules to process an interrupt-driven program and verification parameters, ultimately producing a nondeterministic bounded SP that simulates the execution of the input program. Finally, we utilize CBMC (v5.6) as the verification backend for NIChecker to analyze the resulting SP.

NIChecker reuses the *inline* and *unwind* modules of Lazy-CSeq to preprocess interrupt-driven programs. To better support these programs, we refined the module *instrument* in Python for source-to-source transformations, as described in Section [3.2](#sec-3-2). The instrument module adapts the LazySeq process to the asymmetric concurrent execution typical of interrupt-driven programs.

We developed four independent submodules within the sequentialization process of NIChecker, including *atomicity violation*, *slicing*, LA, and *PPR*. Both LA and atomicity violation modules are implemented in Python. The LA module takes an inlined interrupt-driven program as input and outputs an abstract program to the unwind module. The atomicity violation module takes a global variable $v$, a *pattern*, and an SP as input and outputs an SP with auxiliary code. The modules slicing and PPR, written in Java, perform data flow analysis \[[^44]\] on the input program. Specifically, the slicing module uses a slicing criterion involving global variable $v$ to process an inlined program, while the PPR module identifies and removes replaceable preemption points by analyzing dependencies among global variables across different concurrent flows.

## 5 Experiments and Evaluation

We performed an empirical study to answer the following research questions:

- *RQ1*: *What is the effectiveness of NIChecker in contrast to other state-of-the-art tools (such as Rchecker \[[^20]\], intAtom \[[^30]\], CPA4AV \[[^49]\], and iCBMC \[[^31]\])?*
- *RQ2*: *What is the capability of NIChecker for detecting atomicity violations in real-world interrupt-driven programs?*
- *RQ3*: *What is the contribution of combining LA, slicing, and PPR with NIChecker?*

### 5.1 Experimental Setup

*Benchmarks.* We evaluated NIChecker on the following benchmarks: an academic benchmark (Benchmark 1), a set of real-world interrupt-driven programs (Benchmark 2), and a variant of Benchmark 2 called Benchmark 2 $$.

*Benchmark 1.* An open-source academic benchmark *Racebench [^4]* comprises 31 interrupt-driven C programs artificially designed based on actual aerospace-embedded software. It contains 54 manual atomicity violations covering various manifestations of atomicity violations, such as four harmful access interleaving patterns, shared data types, etc. Each case comprises a main task and 1 to 3 ISRs, with up to three priority levels. Although these cases are relatively short, the corresponding structures are complex enough to pose a challenge for atomicity violation analysis tools. In some cases, one needs to unfold loops in a program 10,000 times to find bugs, which makes the verification tasks more difficult. Another reason that we chose this benchmark is that *Racebench* has been widely used in the evaluation of state-of-the-art tools, such as CPA4AV \[[^49]\], Rchecker \[[^20]\], and intAtom \[[^30]\].

*Benchmark 2.* As shown in [Table 2](#tab2), Benchmark 2 consists of 18 interrupt-driven programs derived from six real-world embedded software packages. For each package, there are three versions, each with a different number of interrupts and different interrupt priorities. These programs range in size from a few hundred to several thousand lines of code. They are all available online and have been previously verified in studies \[[^31], [^41], [^49]\].

#### 5.2.2 User-Defined Assertion Detection.

iCBMC \[[^31]\] is the only open-source, BMC-based tool we know to perform well on Benchmark 2. We compared its capabilities against NIChecker in detecting user-defined assertions within this benchmark. ## 6 Discussion

In this section, we prove the bounded correctness of our LazySeq method and the validity of atomicity violation verification. After that, we further discuss the limitations of our tool and threats to validity.

### 6.1 Bounded Correctness

*Notations and Assumptions.* We assume that $Q = \left\{t a s k , I S R_{1} , \ldots , I S R_{n}\right\}$ denotes an interrupt-driven program consisting of a main task and $n$ ISRs. We recall that the basic LazySeq is composed of two phases as shown in Figure [5](#fig5). In the first phase, the interrupt-driven program $Q$ is translated into a BIDP (Section [3.2.1](#sec-3-2-1)). We obtain a BIDP $P$ with $\left\{f_{0} , f_{1} , \ldots , f_{n}\right\}$ from the interrupt-driven program $Q$ by inlining, unwinding, and bounding the execution times of each ISR with a positive number $ℓ$. Without loss of generality, we assume $ℓ = 1$ [^6] in the following proofs, i.e., the execution of each ISR is simulated at most once. A symbol $\rho$ denotes a fixed sequence of $f_{0} f_{1} \ldots f_{n}$ as defined in Section [3.2.1](#sec-3-2-1). In the second phase, the bounded program $P$ is translated into an SP $$ with $$ (Section [3.2.2](#sec-3-2-2)). Let $\Theta$ denote the set of execution behaviors of a program.

*Proof Strategy.* Since the program $P$ is generated from the program $Q$ by standard bounded translations \[[^16]\], any *k* -round-robin execution of $P$ can be considered as a subset of the execution of $Q$, i.e., $\Theta_{P} \subseteq \Theta_{Q}$. ### 6.2 Limitations

NIChecker currently has the following limitations, and future work will be required to improve it to handle these problems.

*Access Interleaving Patterns.* Atomicity violations defined in terms of access interleaving patterns have not yet accurately described the programmer's intention. Although access interleaving patterns are the most common and fundamental format used to detect atomicity violations, not all such patterns can lead to harmful atomicity violations. They can lead to benign atomicity violations, such as unserializable interleavings, which are necessary to ensure the liveness of certain flag-based while loops. In addition, access interleaving patterns currently only capture atomicity violations involving a single variable, not multiple variables. A more precise format for describing harmful atomicity violations could potentially help NIChecker to address these issues in the future.

*Multiple Types of Interrupts.* Our tool focuses on detecting random interrupts, which can occur at an arbitrary preemption point of a program. However, for real-world embedded software systems, the occurrence of interrupts may be determined by time or other conditions (e.g., timing interrupts and event-triggered interrupts) rather than being entirely random. Therefore, focusing exclusively on random interrupts during the detection process might yield results that slightly deviate from the actual behavior of the program.

*Runtime Overhead.* Like other bounded detection tools, the experimental hit rate of NIChecker increases with the increase of the unwinding depth. However, high precision comes at the cost of increased time consumption due to the enormous state space resulting from a larger unwinding bound. We have also recorded the runtime overhead for sequentialization and validation during our experiments; as shown in [Table 5](#tab5), NIChecker spends approximately 78.5% of the total runtime on LazySeq. These findings suggest that future work should focus on improving efficiency by optimizing the sequentialization process.

### 6.3 Threats to Validity

The study presented in this article has several threats that could potentially limit the validity of the results. In what follows, we discuss these threats along with our mitigation strategies.

*Threats to Internal Validity*. The primary threat to internal validity is potential errors in the implementation. We based the implementation of the basic modules of NIChecker on the source code of Lazy-CSeq, which has been rigorously evaluated in multiple benchmarks of SV-COMP \[[^7], [^8], [^9]\]. This suggests that NIChecker may achieve greater stability in experiments. To further ensure the accuracy of the experimental results, we conducted multiple tests on our benchmarks.

*Threats to External Validity*. Threats to external validity relate to the generalizability of our experimental results. The primary threat to external validity is the benchmarks used in our experiments. The benchmark *Racebench* (Benchmark 1) provides various manifestations of atomicity violations that are artificially designed based on real-world aerospace embedded software characteristics \[[^30]\] and widely used in other studies. On the other hand, to further enhance the generalizability of our results, we also chose 18 real-world interrupt-driven programs (Benchmark 2), which is widely used in certain state-of-the-art studies \[[^31], [^41], [^49]\], to evaluate NIChecker.

*Threats to Construct Validity*. Threats to construct validity relate to the suitability of our experimental evaluation. To mitigate the threat, we use metrics consisting of *precision*, *hit rate*, *FP rate*, *runtime*, *memory*, and *number of variables and clauses* to evaluate NIChecker. These metrics have been widely used in related works, as demonstrated in \[[^20], [^30], [^31], [^36]\].

## 7 Related Work

Several studies have been conducted on sequentialization for detecting data races in interrupt-driven programs \[[^11], [^40], [^48]\]. Wu et al. \[[^48]\] introduced a data race detection method for interrupt-driven programs through sequentialization and CBMC. This method generates an SP by inserting the nondeterministic scheduling of interrupts into all possible preemption points of an interrupt-driven program. The resulting SP is then detected using CBMC. This method suffers from the state-explore problem when analyzing large-scale programs or programs containing large loops. Techniques like \[[^11], [^40]\] combine sequentialization with partial order reduction to minimize the number of unnecessary interrupt calls, aiming at reducing the state space. They do not handle nested execution of interrupts at multiple priority levels. Inverso et al. \[[^24] – [^26]\] proposed a LazySeq technique that uses a context-bounded technique to limit the detection state space of concurrent programs. This technique fits well with BMC but inevitably becomes inefficient when finding deep bugs. It also doesn’t work well for interrupt-driven programs because of the differences between interrupts and threads. In contrast, our work not only adapts the LazySeq for nested interrupts but also introduces a refined LA technique \[[^17]\] specifically designed to improve the scalability of LazySeq. NIChecker takes advantage of LazySeq and thus can generate SSA formulas of smaller sizes than those produced by other sequentialization techniques.

Kroening et al. \[[^27]\] and Liang et al. \[[^31]\] presented a bounded verification tool iCBMC that verifies user-defined assertions in interrupt-driven programs. iCBMC directly encodes the nested interleaving constraints of interrupts into the SSA formula and solves this formula using an SAT/SMT solver. This tool does not automatically detect atomicity violations in interrupt-driven programs. Regehr \[[^39]\] has proposed to verify interrupts via a thread-aware verification tool that models threads with static priorities. There are also several model checking techniques \[[^32], [^37]\] that model interrupt-driven programs with specific modeling languages. Program analysts need to be proficient with modeling language using these techniques, while our tool only requires several simple parameters to detect bugs automatically and is thus more user-friendly. Techniques like \[[^41]\] employed an abstract interpretation framework to verify interrupt-driven programs. This technique aims to prove the absence of bugs rather than find bugs.

Several static analysis approaches have been proposed \[[^30], [^49]\] to find potential atomicity violations in interrupt-driven programs. Li et al. \[[^30]\] proposed a static analysis tool, intAtom, to detect atomicity violations of interrupt-driven programs. intAtom first uses a pattern-matching algorithm to find all potential atomicity violations. Then, it performs local reachability analysis to find false violations whose paths between two consecutive accesses are infeasible. Although a lot of FPs can be eliminated in this way, intAtom still requires a lot of manual effort to confirm which of its results are real violations. A configuration tool, CPA4AV, proposed by Yu et al. \[[^49]\], relies on BDD-based reachability computation but is unable to analyze complex data types.

Previous work \[[^19] – [^20]\] has attempted to combine static analysis and verification for concurrency bug detection. The basic idea of these methods is to find all potential bugs with static analysis and verify whether these bugs are reachable using CBMC. However, they may also suffer the state explosion problem when verifying deep bugs using CBMC.

Dynamic analysis techniques based on testing have been proposed \[[^14], [^23], [^28], [^38], [^42], [^43], [^51]\] to detect data races in interrupt-driven programs. Regehr \[[^38]\] designed a restricted interrupt discipline to strengthen the embedded software concerning unexpected interrupts, which makes random testing leveraged possible to find interrupt-related bugs in interrupt-driven programs. Compared to random testing presented in \[[^38]\], Higashi et al. \[[^23]\] introduced a testing approach that accounts for all potential timing scenarios involving interrupts. Nevertheless, when testing multiple interrupt handlers, users are required to apply their method individually to each interrupt. Lai et al. \[[^28]\] proposed two test adequacy criteria based on inter-context data flows and inter-context control flows, respectively, to detect more failures in the NesC language. Yu et al. \[[^51]\] introduced a framework that provides observability and fine-grained controllability features to allow test engineers to detect concurrency faults caused by interactions between software and hardware. Chopra et al. \[[^14]\] presented a definition of data races and a happens-before ordering for interrupt-driven programs and detected races on the FreeRTOS kernel library. In summary, these dynamic analysis approaches are inadequate for debugging interrupt-driven programs since they can only identify the existence of bugs but fail to replicate them accurately.

Wang et al. \[[^45] – [^46]\] proposed a hybrid approach based on static analysis and dynamic simulation to detect data races in interrupt-driven programs. This approach uses static analysis to identify potential race warnings and generates input data for exercising these warnings through symbolic execution. The remaining race conditions identified from symbolic execution are dynamically validated in a virtual environment. Yu et al. \[[^50]\] introduced a combined technique that first identifies synchronization candidates with static analysis and then refines them using an industrial full-system simulation platform. These combined techniques focus solely on data race detection and do not address atomicity violations.

## 8 Conclusion

We present a tool NIChecker that detects atomicity violations and user-defined assertions of interrupt-driven programs. The tool translates an interrupt-driven program into an SP using LazySeq, and then the backend CBMC detects the resulting SP. We propose an algorithm to detect atomicity violations automatically. Additionally, we propose a refined LA for handling programs that contain loops with large or unknown loop bounds and introduce slicing and PPR to reduce the state space of the SP. Theoretically, we prove the bounded correctness of our translation. We evaluate NIChecker using 31 academic benchmark programs and 18 real-world interrupt-driven programs, comparing it with the most related and state-of-the-art tools. The experimental results indicate that the proposed NIChecker achieves the highest accuracy and the lowest FPs compared to existing tools. The proposed optimization methods have improved the hit rate of detection results and the verification speed of the backend.

In this article, we focus on detecting atomicity violations that are more complex and the most encountered concurrency bugs in interrupt-driven programs.
